# Leadership Language Practice

> Added 2026-09-17, from Darryl, following the David Marquet material in the Wesco Leading Managers program (month 1). Source thinking: intent-based leadership, leader-leader rather than leader-follower.
> Status: **in practice by Darryl from 2026-09-17.** Not yet a stated expectation of the team, and not a process. Do not describe it in any deliverable as a team standard, a program, or an initiative.

## What the practice is

Two changes to the language Darryl uses and reinforces, applied at every level of communication rather than only in written updates.

**1. "We" for the outcome, a named person for the next step.**

The team describes work by sorting it across boundaries: sales asked for this, product owns that, that is a services issue. The statements are usually accurate and they still end the conversation at the boundary. The correction is not to blur ownership, which is a worse failure. It is to carry both in the same sentence: the outcome is shared, the next step has a name.

- Avoid: "That's a sales problem." / "We should probably look at that."
- Use: "The customer isn't getting what they bought, so that's ours to solve. The config change is on us this week; the commercial conversation is theirs."

The test: a sentence that names a boundary invites the question of whose fault it is. A sentence that starts with the shared outcome invites the question of what help is available.

**2. "I intend to" replaces "Can I" and "Do you think we should."**

A question moves the decision up and leaves the thinking down. A stated intent carries the action, the reasoning, and the ownership together, and reduces Darryl's role to confirming, correcting, or asking one question.

- Avoid: "Do you want us to schedule the upgrade before the release?"
- Use: "I intend to schedule the upgrade after the release lands, because the customer's maintenance window closes first. Tell me if you see a reason not to."

**Precondition.** Stated intent is only real where people have the context and the decision rights to form one. Where the team cannot state intent, treat it as evidence that the goal, the constraints, or the decision rights were not made clear, and fix that rather than the phrasing.

## Where it applies

All channels, deliberately. The default language of an organization is set in one-on-ones, small group discussion, and hallway conversation, not in written updates or announcements. Asking for stated intent in a team forum and then answering a direct one-on-one question with "let me think about it and get back to you" teaches the opposite rule.

Highest-value settings, in order: escalations; customer commitments made by the team; design and release decisions; cross-functional asks to Sales, Marketing, Managed Services, and Support.

## What Claude does with this

When drafting or editing anything in Darryl's voice:

- Prefer stated intent over a request for permission where Darryl is the one acting. "I intend to X, unless you see a reason not to" rather than "Would it make sense to X?"
- In cross-functional messages, open on the shared outcome, then name the owner of each next step. Do not leave an ask floating with no name against it.
- Do not use "we" as a substitute for an owner. If a sentence uses "we" for a step rather than an outcome, flag it.
- Keep this consistent with `Communication_Preferences.md` §3: courteous, not deferential; no softening that understates the priority of Darryl's ask.
- This does not override the lighter register for genuine status-check nudges.

## Watch items

- Adoption is Darryl's own first. Model before asking.
- Risk: "I intend to" adopted as a phrase without the judgment behind it. The signal is intent statements with no reasoning attached.
- Risk: "we" used to avoid naming an owner. The signal is shared-outcome language on items that are stalled with nobody chasing them.
- Sales partner language is outside Darryl's authority to change directly. The only mechanism is example.
