# Benefits Realization and Executive Reporting

**Version:** 1.0  
**Last updated:** 2026-07-14  
**Sensitivity:** Internal

## 1. Purpose

Benefits realization connects product, data, platform, and service work to measurable business outcomes.

Darryl's executive reporting increasingly requires a clear distinction between planned benefits, targeted benefits, realized benefits, leading indicators, dependencies, evidence, commercial impact, operational impact, and customer impact.

## 2. Core Principle

A delivered feature is not automatically a realized benefit.

Examples:

- An integration go-live is a realized capability milestone.
- Reduced manual effort is realized only when measured.
- A release cadence is a delivery mechanism.
- Customer reliability or commercial growth must be evidenced separately.

## 3. Standard Benefit Structure

### Benefit Definition

A plain-language statement of the intended business outcome.

### Targeted Benefits

The improvements the initiative is designed to create.

### Realized Benefit Progress

Evidence already achieved, including capability milestones and measured outcomes.

### Planned Benefits

Expected future outcomes that still depend on adoption, data, process, or commercial execution.

### Dependencies

The conditions required for the benefit to materialize.

## 4. Benefits Register Fields

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Initiative | Product, service, or program |
| Benefit | Specific outcome |
| Benefit type | Revenue, margin, customer, risk, productivity, strategic capability |
| Baseline | Starting measure |
| Target | Desired measure |
| Leading indicator | Early evidence |
| Realized evidence | Confirmed result |
| Owner | Accountable person |
| Dependency | Required enabler |
| Confidence | Strength of evidence |

## 8. Executive Slide Standard

Primary slide text should normally include a concise benefit sentence.

Speaker notes may use:

- Benefit definition
- Targeted benefits
- Realized benefit progress
- Planned benefits

Do not overload the slide with the full methodology.

## 9. Common Reporting Errors

- Calling activity a benefit
- Using aspirational language without evidence
- Mixing realized and planned benefits
- Reporting technical output without business implication
- Using one metric for multiple distinct benefits
- Failing to name the owner
- Ignoring adoption
- Claiming commercial impact without sales or customer evidence
- Treating absence of evidence as zero benefit
