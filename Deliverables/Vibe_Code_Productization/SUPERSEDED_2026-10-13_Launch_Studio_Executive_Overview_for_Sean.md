## The problem

The business knows its own problems and, with AI-assisted building, can now prototype solutions itself. What it cannot do is take a prototype to production: security, identity, hosting, release, support and lifecycle. Today a good prototype either stays a personal tool, drifts into use on real data with nobody owning it, or dies. Across the industry the pattern is the same: adoption is near universal and measured value is rare, and the tools that do reach production without an owner become an engineering burden.

## What Launch Studio is

A path with three lanes and no new approval body.

| Lane | Who | What they do |
|--|------|------|
| Find and prove | Mike Wassil and the AI Builders Guild | Surface problems, coach safe prototypes in the sandbox, gather user proof, bring ideas forward |
| Grade, build and run | Launch Studio: Darryl Benson and Ron Snyder (leads), David Blondel (product owner), Ron's engineering team | Run the on-ramp: grade each idea for business value and prototype viability; then build to standard, host, secure, support and eventually retire |
| Decide | The bodies that exist today: AI Council and intake, Security and WAB, Finance, the business sponsor | Approve, fund and validate; review depth matched to risk |

Four principles make it work:

- **The builder sees a studio; IT carries the governance.** Builders submit once, hear back with a reason, and stay involved through demos and acceptance. They never fill in a governance form or touch production code. If IT absorbs the compliance work, compliance goes up and friction goes down.
- **If IT touches it, IT owns it.** The business cannot own applications. A prototype is the business's; a product is IT's. There is no halfway tier.
- **Choose the build route, do not rebuild by default.** Reuse an existing tool, extend one, buy, harden the prototype, or rebuild. Every route passes the same release tests.
- **Count money, not activity.** Every product signs a one-page value contract before build: the number it should move, the baseline, the proof method and the stop rule. Finance reviews it. Value is reported in separate lines and never added into one figure.

## The on-ramp

Launch Studio fronts the path with one inlet filter, so that engineering time is only spent on ideas that have earned it. Every idea arrives the same way and is graded on two questions, using a short set of tools rather than a committee:

| Question | What we grade | Tools |
|--|------|------|
| Is it worth doing? | Business value: the number it should move, the baseline, the mechanism, who sponsors it and who uses it | One-page intake; a value score reviewed with Finance; a check for overlap with tools we already run |
| Can it become a product? | Prototype viability: what data it touches, what systems it connects to, how it would be built (reuse, extend, buy, harden, rebuild), what it would cost to run | Risk tier check; a short engineering review of the prototype; a sized estimate for build and upkeep |

Every submission gets one of four answers, with the reason: take it in, fold it into an existing product, defer it, or return it to the sandbox. The target is an answer within 30 days. The grade is also what the sponsor signs up to: the value score becomes the value contract, and the sized estimate becomes the project that reimburses IT.

## Capacity

Launch Studio decides what it takes into build, against a work-in-progress limit Ron sets. The business decides what it funds; the approval bodies decide what is allowed; we decide what our team commits to. Starting capacity is the current team. More people is a decision for later, once there is evidence.

## Funding

The mechanism is a future decision with Finance. The shape: IT funds the standing team, and each product is a business-funded project that reimburses IT for productization and then for upkeep, through the chargeback that already exists. The business chooses from a short menu so the decision is easy to make:

| Option | The business commits | IT carries |
|--|------|------|
| Sandbox | Nothing; prototype stays on non-production data | Registration and visibility |
| Pilot | A bounded project with a 90-day decision | Build to pilot standard, measurement |
| Sponsored product | Reimbursement for productization, then annual upkeep | Full ownership, end to end |
| Shared product | Co-funded by the segments that use it | Full ownership plus reuse across segments |

One rule holds regardless of mechanism: no sponsor, no production.

## Pilot

Each quarter ends with evidence, not a date: a signed charter and candidate baselines by year end, then first measured results or stop decisions in Q1. If the gates hold, open the path to EES, UBS and the functions in 2027.
