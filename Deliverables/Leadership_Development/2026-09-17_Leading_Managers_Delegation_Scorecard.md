## Question 1

As you reflect on the statements in the delegation scorecard, what strengths do you recognize in your own delegation approach?

### Reply (draft)

My highest marks land in one place, and it is the part of the job I enjoy most: getting a group aligned on what matters and then letting them run at it.

Specifically, I involve the team in defining the process and the outcome rather than handing over a finished plan. I make sure people know their own role and everyone else's, and we agree up front who needs to be consulted and why. When something goes wrong, I give them a chance to work it out before I step in. Those depend on knowing what each person is actually good at, which I do.

Underneath it is something I learned early: what gets measured gets done. Stating the goal clearly, and making success matter to the job rather than optional, is not pressure. It is the alternative to people showing up day after day without knowing whether they are winning.

The common thread is that I am at my best delegating to a team. That is worth knowing, because it is not the same skill as delegating to a person.

## Question 2

As you reflect on the statements in the delegation scorecard, what challenges have you identified in your delegation approach that you might address?

### Reply (draft v2)

My lower scores are all one-to-one items: delegating to develop someone rather than to get the work done reliably, briefing purpose, setting accountability expectations, and recognizing the work afterward.

The hard version shows up with experienced people. Some read past success as evidence they do not need to change, and it comes out as resistance to teaming or to admitting when something went wrong. My instinct is to route around it, either by giving the assignment to whoever will take it or by covering the part they will not.

That is the challenge, and it is mine rather than theirs. Routing around resistance protects the delivery and leaves the person where they were.

What I would change: name the expectation and the accountability up front, and say the purpose out loud instead of assuming experience supplies it. Experience is not the same as context.

The third is recognition. If I only surface someone's work when it is off track, I am measuring in one direction.
