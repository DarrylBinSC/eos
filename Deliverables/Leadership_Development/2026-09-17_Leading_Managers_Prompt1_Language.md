# Leading Managers: Writing Prompt 1, Changing the Language of Leadership

> Cohort: Wesco Leading Managers (program contact: Lynnda West). Month 1 material: David Marquet podcast.
> Status: **Draft v2, 2026-09-17.** Shortened from v1 and tightened to Darryl's voice. Audience: facilitator and cohort. Examples unattributed.

## Prompt

Marquet spoke at length about changing the language of how we lead. He explained his approach to turning passive followers into active leaders by eliminating passive phrases such as "Do you think we should..." or "Could we...". Instead, he taught his crew to use active, empowered phrases such as "I intend to." How might a similar approach impact the work your team executes?

## Reply (draft v2, submission candidate)

Two changes to how I use language came out of the Marquet material. The first is the one I did not expect.

My team is precise about ownership. When we discuss a customer problem or a capability sales is asking for, the sentence sorts the work by who is responsible: sales asked for this, product owns that, that one belongs to services. Those statements are usually accurate, which is what makes them hard to correct. They also end the conversation at a boundary, and the problem is usually on the other side of it.

The fix is not to blur ownership. "We should probably look at that" is worse than "that belongs to sales," because at least the second one names someone. I want both in the same sentence: "we" for the outcome, a named person for the next step. The customer is not getting what they bought, so it is our problem. The configuration change is ours this week; the commercial conversation is theirs.

That matters because nothing in my portfolio is finished by one function. A capability creates value only when sales can sell it, services can deliver it, support can hold it, and the customer adopts it. If our language stops at our boundary, so does our understanding of what the rest of the business needs from us.

This is not only my team's habit. Our sales partners describe the platform as something my organization does rather than something we sell together. I cannot change their language by asking for it. I can change ours and see whether it pulls.

The second change is the verb. "Can I" and "do you think we should" both move the decision to me and leave the thinking with the person asking. That makes me the constraint. "I intend to schedule the upgrade after the release lands, because the customer's maintenance window closes first" gives me the action, the reasoning and the ownership in one sentence, and my job becomes confirming, correcting, or asking one question. I expect it to matter most in three places: escalations, customer commitments made without me, and release decisions.

The harder part is consistency. If I ask for this in team meetings and then answer a one-on-one question with "let me think about it and get back to you," I have taught the real rule, which is that decisions still come back to me. It has to hold in small groups, in one-on-ones, and in the way I write, or it does not hold at all.

One condition is worth stating. Intent only works where people have the context and the authority to form one. Marquet paired the language change with competence and organizational clarity. If my team cannot state an intent, that is usually evidence that I have not been clear enough about the goal, the constraints, or who decides.

What I am after is speed. Problems get solved closest to where they are seen, and they get solved once.

## Superseded draft v1 (2026-09-17, approximately 900 words)

The language change I most need to make on my team is not the one I expected going in. Marquet's "I intend to" is the memorable phrase, and I will come back to it, but the word I keep catching is "we."

My team is precise about ownership. When we talk about a customer problem, a stalled deployment, or a capability sales is asking for, the sentence usually sorts the world into who is responsible: sales asked for this, the customer wants that, product owns that decision, that one belongs to services. Most of those statements are accurate, which is what makes them hard to correct. But accuracy is not the same as usefulness. Each of those sentences ends the conversation at a boundary, and the problem is usually sitting on the other side of it.

The alternative is not to blur ownership. Blurred ownership is its own failure, and "we should probably look at that" is a worse sentence than "that belongs to sales," because at least the second one names someone. What I want is both: "we" for the outcome, and a named person for the next step. The customer's system is not doing what they bought it to do, so that is our problem. The configuration change is ours to schedule and the commercial conversation is theirs to have. Same facts, different starting point, and a different second question. When the sentence starts at a boundary, the next question is whose fault it is. When it starts with "we," the next question is what help is available.

This matters commercially more than it sounds. Almost nothing in my portfolio is finished by one function. A platform capability creates value only when sales can sell it, marketing can describe it, services can deliver it, support can hold it, and the customer actually adopts it. If my team's default language stops at our boundary, our empathy stops there too, and we end up building things that are technically correct and commercially inert. Work can satisfy every requirement it was given and still not land, and when that happens it is usually because nobody outside engineering was in the sentence when the work was described.

I should be honest that this is not only my team's habit. Our sales partners talk about the platform the same way, as something my organization does rather than something we sell together. I cannot fix their language by asking for it. I can change ours and see whether it pulls. Leading by example is the only mechanism I have across an organizational line, and it is not a weak one. If my team consistently describes shared outcomes as shared, and stays direct about what is ours, the ask becomes easier to make and harder to refuse.

Now the verb. "Can I..." and "Do you think we should..." do the same thing. They move the decision to me and leave the thinking with the person who asked. That is comfortable for everyone and it makes me the constraint. "I intend to schedule the upgrade after the release lands, because the customer's maintenance window closes first" gives me the action, the reasoning, and the ownership in one sentence, and it reduces my job to confirming, correcting, or asking one question. It is faster for them and cheaper for me. More importantly, it is a rehearsal. Someone who has to say what they intend has already had to decide.

For the development, product, and deployment teams I lead, I expect this to show up in three places first. Escalations, where "what do you want us to do" costs hours. Customer commitments, where I often hear about the commitment after it has been made. Design and release decisions, where I would rather hear an intent I can correct than a question I have to answer.

The part I think is easy to underestimate is consistency. If I ask for this in team meetings and written updates, then answer a direct question in a one-on-one with "let me think about it and get back to you," I have taught the real rule, which is that decisions still come back to me. The default language of an organization is set in small rooms, not in announcements. It has to hold in one-on-ones, in small group discussions, in the hallway version of the conversation, and in the way I write. That is the harder commitment, and it is mine before it is anyone else's.

There is one condition worth naming. "I intend to" works only when people have the context and the authority to form an intent worth stating. Marquet paired the language change with technical competence and organizational clarity, and without those the phrase becomes theater, people announcing intentions they are not equipped to have. So the change puts the first obligation on me. If my team cannot state intent, that is usually evidence that I have not made the goal, the constraints, or the decision rights clear enough.

The payoff I am after is speed. Problems get solved closest to where they are seen, and they get solved once. Accountability and a shared sense of team do more for that than any process change I could introduce this quarter.
